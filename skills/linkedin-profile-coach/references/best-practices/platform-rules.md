# Platform rules

The audit uses these rules in every checklist row and for the name fields, and the write phase uses them in every draft and in `drafts/settings.md`. The conventions (tiers, the ≈ dates, "Inference:") are explained in [best practices](../best-practices.md).

## Platform rules (`rules-`)

These rules hold what LinkedIn's terms and policies require of the profile and of how it is edited and used in a job search; every phase follows `rules-1` and `rules-6`, and setup, audit and write apply the rest.

- `rules-1` **Let no software read or act on LinkedIn for the person.** The skill reads the profile only from what the person provides (their LinkedIn data download, a Save to PDF copy, screenshots or pasted text) and opens no profile or signed-in LinkedIn page itself, even when a browser tool is at hand. The write phase hands over texts and settings that the person enters by hand. When the person asks about scrapers, browser extensions, bots, auto-apply tools or AI agents that work in their account, explain that LinkedIn bans software that scrapes or automates activity on its site, and that members who use it risk having their account restricted or shut down.
  - Why: The User Agreement in force on 2026-10-30 is the version effective 2025-11-03. LinkedIn's page of updates lists no change to it in 2026. Section 8.2 is a list of 19 items, and these rules number them in page order. Item 2 bans developing, supporting or using software, scripts, robots or similar means, such as crawlers, browser plugins and add-ons, to scrape or copy LinkedIn, profiles included. Item 13 bans bots and other unauthorized automated methods that access LinkedIn, add or download contacts, send messages, or create, comment on, like or share posts. LinkedIn Help says it does not allow third-party software or browser extensions that scrape, change the look of, or automate activity on its website. It says members who use such tools risk having their accounts restricted or shut down. Its page on account restrictions adds that automated inauthentic activity can lead to a temporary or permanent restriction. LinkedIn Help also says its daily and speed limits on Easy Apply are partly meant to curb automation and bots; `settings-9` holds those limits. Inference: an auto-apply tool that submits applications on LinkedIn, or an AI agent or agentic browser that works in the account, is software that automates activity on the site, so items 2 and 13 cover it as written. No LinkedIn page names AI agents or agentic browsers, so the skill tells the person that this is its reading of the ban. LinkedIn offers its own ways to get the profile: Download your data, and Save to PDF, which works on desktop when the profile language and the language setting are both English. Its Privacy Policy gives members the right to ask for a machine-readable copy of the personal data they provided. This rule is the basis of the skill's hand-paste rule.
  - Source: [User Agreement](https://www.linkedin.com/legal/user-agreement), LinkedIn, effective 2025-11-03, tier 1; [Updates to User Agreement and Privacy Policy](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a1341216/updates-to-user-agreement-and-privacy-policy), LinkedIn Help Center, updated ≈2026-08, tier 1; [Automated activity on LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/90586), LinkedIn Help Center, updated ≈2026-10, tier 1; [Prohibited software and extensions](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a1341387), LinkedIn Help Center, updated ≈2024, tier 1; [Account restrictions](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a1340522), LinkedIn Help Center, updated ≈2025, tier 1; [Apply to jobs directly on LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a512348), LinkedIn Help Center, updated ≈2025, tier 1; [Download your data](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a1339364/downloading-your-account-data), LinkedIn Help Center, updated ≈2026-10, tier 1; [Save a profile as a PDF](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a541960), LinkedIn Help Center, updated ≈2026-10, tier 1; [LinkedIn Privacy Policy](https://www.linkedin.com/legal/privacy-policy), LinkedIn, effective 2025-11-03, tier 1.
- `rules-2` **Make every claim and every affiliation on the profile true.** The audit checks each claim in the current profile, from titles, dates and results to skills, certifications and the organizations named, against the evidence records and `progress.md`. A claim that overstates its evidence gets `needs work`, and a claim with no evidence yet becomes a question for `work-history-harvest`. The write phase takes every claim from an approved evidence record, and section rules apply this one, such as `experience-4` to positions and `career-break-1` to the end of the last role.
  - Why: In the User Agreement, section 3.1, members promise that their profile will be truthful. The Professional Community Policies forbid posting misleading or deceptive information about your qualifications, work experience, affiliations or achievements. They also forbid associating yourself with a business or organization you are not professionally associated with, and they do not define that tie. LinkedIn Help says untruthful information breaks both documents. It could lead other members to flag the profile as suspicious, which could result in restrictions to the account. LinkedIn may also restrict an account whose profile, or part of it, is intentionally fraudulent. Its page on account restrictions names the name, the profile and background photos, and Experience or Education among the profile content that must follow its policies. LinkedIn's accuracy page asks members to update their profile each time they change jobs. Its search help warns against artificially inflating the Experience and Education fields. Its Talent Blog says to list only skills you actually have. The User Agreement adds that LinkedIn may use member data to recommend members to recruiters, and that an accurate, up-to-date profile makes those recommendations more accurate and relevant. The skill's evidence promise meets this rule and goes further: every claim traces to an evidence record and stays defensible in an interview.
  - Source: [User Agreement](https://www.linkedin.com/legal/user-agreement), LinkedIn, effective 2025-11-03, tier 1; [LinkedIn Professional Community Policies](https://www.linkedin.com/legal/professional-community-policies), LinkedIn, undated, read 2026-10-30, tier 1; [Representing Yourself Accurately on Your Profile](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a1337379), LinkedIn Help Center, updated ≈2022, tier 1; [Account restrictions](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a1340522), LinkedIn Help Center, updated ≈2025, tier 1; [Practices to avoid when optimizing your profile for LinkedIn search](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a525117), LinkedIn Help Center, updated ≈2023, tier 1; [Why It's Important to List Skills on Your LinkedIn Profile](https://www.linkedin.com/business/talent/blog/talent-acquisition/skills-on-linkedin-profile), LinkedIn Talent Blog, 2024-04-04, tier 1.
- `rules-3` **Put on the profile only what the person has the right to share.** The audit marks confidential detail in the current profile, such as a client name, an internal figure, an internal system name or an unreleased product, `needs work`, `high`. It rates the same way any file, image or video, media attached to positions included, that belongs to an employer, a client or anyone else, and asks the person whenever ownership is unclear. The write phase runs the NDA pass over every draft and proposes only the person's own public work, such as a published article, a public talk or open-source code, or material its owner has published.
  - Why: In the User Agreement, section 3.1, members promise to provide only content and information they have the right to share and that violates no one's rights, intellectual property included. Section 8.2 item 5 forbids disclosing information you do not have the consent to disclose, such as confidential information of others, including your employer. Item 6 forbids violating the intellectual property rights of others, including copyrights, patents, trademarks and trade secrets. LinkedIn's Copyright Policy says LinkedIn may disable or terminate the accounts of members who infringe, or repeatedly infringe, the rights of others. LinkedIn Help says an uploaded cover image must comply with the User Agreement too. Item 5 names the employer and says nothing about former employers. Inference: after a layoff, the person's own contract or NDA decides what stays confidential and which work material they may show, so the NDA pass applies the extra rules that setup records. The skill's clean rule covers these clauses and goes further: it also leaves out names and details that are not secret but would let a reader recognize a client, a colleague or an internal system. `featured-1`, `photo-5` and `sections-6` apply this rule to Featured, the cover image and Projects.
  - Source: [User Agreement](https://www.linkedin.com/legal/user-agreement), LinkedIn, effective 2025-11-03, tier 1; [Copyright Policy](https://www.linkedin.com/legal/copyright-policy), LinkedIn, effective 2014-03-26, tier 1; [Add or change the cover image on your profile](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a568217), LinkedIn Help Center, updated ≈2026-03, tier 1.
- `rules-4` **Keep the name fields to the person's name.** The audit rates anything in the name fields besides the name and optional pronouns, such as a job title, a keyword, "Open to Work", a company, an email address or symbols, `needs work`, `high`. A credential after the name, such as a degree or a certification, gets `needs work`, `medium`, and the person decides once they know the conflict described below. The write phase adds nothing to the name fields and moves titles and keywords into the headline draft, and credentials into Education or Licenses and certifications.
  - Why: LinkedIn Help says the name fields may only include the first, middle and last names of the member's real or preferred professional name, plus their preferred pronouns. LinkedIn's page on search practices is stricter: the name fields must reflect only the first and last name, with no extra information. The two pages differ on middle names and pronouns; the skill follows the wider one, since both keep titles, keywords, company names and email addresses out. The User Agreement asks members to use their real name on their profile (section 8.1) and to hold one account in their real name (section 2.1). LinkedIn's people-search help says a profile that appears overly optimized may be affected by spam detection. Inference: keywords in the name fields are the plainest case of that. On credentials the pages conflict. LinkedIn's accuracy page says members can add a former name, a nickname, preferred pronouns and professional certifications, but it names no field, while the page on names allows only names and pronouns. Both pages are about four years old, and LinkedIn does not say which one it enforces. The skill follows the page on names and leaves the final choice to the person.
  - Source: [Names allowed on profiles](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a1337288), LinkedIn Help Center, updated ≈2022, tier 1; [Practices to avoid when optimizing your profile for LinkedIn search](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a525117), LinkedIn Help Center, updated ≈2023, tier 1; [User Agreement](https://www.linkedin.com/legal/user-agreement), LinkedIn, effective 2025-11-03, tier 1; [LinkedIn Search relevance for people search](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a524188), LinkedIn Help Center, updated ≈2026-03, tier 1; [Representing Yourself Accurately on Your Profile](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a1337379), LinkedIn Help Center, updated ≈2022, tier 1.
- `rules-5` **Have the person review, edit and approve every draft before it goes on LinkedIn.** The write phase builds each draft from evidence records and the person's own phrasing, and keeps the current text beside it so the person can go back. If the person asks whether to disclose that AI helped, give LinkedIn's recommendation and leave the decision to them.
  - Why: The User Agreement, section 3.6, says content on LinkedIn may be generated with tools offered by LinkedIn or by others off LinkedIn. Section 3.6 is the only place the agreement mentions AI, and no clause bans AI-assisted text. It asks members to review and edit what LinkedIn's generative AI features produce before sharing it. It also makes members responsible for all content they share complying with the Professional Community Policies, including not sharing misleading information. LinkedIn Help on AI-assisted content urges members to review, edit and approve it. It says the content should reflect the member's own voice, perspective and experience. It recommends telling others, when it is not obvious from the context, that you relied heavily on AI. That page discusses posts and comments, does not mention profiles and gives no way to disclose on a profile. Inference: when the person supplies the facts and edits the text, their use of AI may not count as heavy reliance; the call is theirs. LinkedIn's own Profile Writing Assistance, offered only to a select group of Premium subscribers, recommends editing its suggestions before saving them. Saved suggestions replace the original text, and getting it back means editing by hand. LinkedIn's guidance matches the skill's promise that the person decides, and `style-5`.
  - Source: [User Agreement](https://www.linkedin.com/legal/user-agreement), LinkedIn, effective 2025-11-03, tier 1; [Best practices for content created with the help of AI](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a1481496), LinkedIn Help Center, updated ≈2026-10, tier 1; [Enhance your profile with LinkedIn's Profile Writing Assistance](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a1444194), LinkedIn Help Center, updated ≈2026-08, tier 1; [Enhance your profile with LinkedIn's Profile Writing Assistance FAQ](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a1453665), LinkedIn Help Center, updated ≈2026-07, tier 1.
- `rules-6` **Never take the person's LinkedIn password, a session cookie or access to their account.** If the person offers any of these, decline and say why in one sentence. Draft only for the person who will paste the text into their own profile.
  - Why: The User Agreement, section 8.2 item 1, forbids creating a profile for anyone other than yourself and using another person's account, and gives sharing log-in credentials and copying cookies as examples. Section 2.2 asks members to keep their password confidential and not to share or transfer their account or any part of it. The Professional Community Policies also tell members not to share their LinkedIn account with anyone else. Inference: a tool that signs in with the person's password or cookies puts them in breach of these clauses, on top of `rules-1`.
  - Source: [User Agreement](https://www.linkedin.com/legal/user-agreement), LinkedIn, effective 2025-11-03, tier 1; [LinkedIn Professional Community Policies](https://www.linkedin.com/legal/professional-community-policies), LinkedIn, undated, read 2026-10-30, tier 1.
- `rules-7` **Treat the LinkedIn account as the person's own, and services the employer pays for as the employer's.** Setup asks which LinkedIn services the employer pays for, such as a Recruiter seat or a LinkedIn Learning subscription. It tells the person that the account stays theirs, while the employer controls those services and can get reports on their use. Before access ends, setup asks the person to save what they need from those services, such as the LinkedIn Learning courses they completed, which `work-history-harvest` records.
  - Why: The User Agreement, section 2.2, says that as between the member and others, their employer included, the account belongs to the member. When another party, such as an employer, buys a Service for the member to use in their work, such as a Recruiter seat or a LinkedIn Learning subscription, that party has the right to control access to it and to get reports on the member's use of it. That party has no rights to the personal account. Inference: a seat the employer pays for can end with the job, since the employer controls access to it. The User Agreement does not say what happens then to LinkedIn Learning certificates already on the profile. The account's primary email, which can be lost with the work mailbox, is covered by `settings-1`.
  - Source: [User Agreement](https://www.linkedin.com/legal/user-agreement), LinkedIn, effective 2025-11-03, tier 1.
- `rules-8` **Take only patterns from other members' profiles.** When the person points to a profile they admire, ask what they like about it, such as its order, length or angle, and draft from their own evidence and words. Other members' text stays out of the workspace and the drafts. The audit marks profile text the person says they copied from someone else `needs work`.
  - Why: The User Agreement, section 8.2 item 4, forbids copying, using, displaying or distributing information obtained from LinkedIn, directly or through third parties, without the content owner's consent. Item 6 forbids violating others' intellectual property rights, and its example covers copying others' content without permission. LinkedIn Help on AI-assisted content says content should reflect the member's own voice, perspective and experience. Inference: lifting another member's About or Experience text into a draft is copying of this kind; LinkedIn does not discuss template text. The skill reads no other member's profile on LinkedIn itself, under `rules-1`.
  - Source: [User Agreement](https://www.linkedin.com/legal/user-agreement), LinkedIn, effective 2025-11-03, tier 1; [Best practices for content created with the help of AI](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a1481496), LinkedIn Help Center, updated ≈2026-10, tier 1.
- `rules-9` **Label clearly any synthetic or altered media of a person saying or doing what they did not.** The audit asks whether Featured items, media attached to positions or the cover image include AI-made or altered images or video that show a person saying or doing something they did not, and rates such an item without a clear label `needs work`. The write phase drafts the label for the item's title or description. For an AI-made or AI-edited profile photo, `photo-2` applies, and the person's word on the likeness stands.
  - Why: The Professional Community Policies forbid sharing synthetic or manipulated media that depicts a person saying something they did not say or doing something they did not do, without clearly disclosing its fake or altered nature. The User Agreement, section 8.2 item 18, gives such manipulated media as an example of deceitful use. LinkedIn's summary of its 2025 User Agreement changes lists deep fakes and other wrongful impersonations as prohibited. LinkedIn Help says members can view Content Credentials on image and video content that has C2PA information attached, including a statement on whether AI was used to generate or edit it. That statement comes from whoever created and signed the credential. LinkedIn says it cannot yet identify and label all AI-generated or modified content, and the feature is rolling out gradually. Inference: LinkedIn's own label shows only where a file carries this data, so the person's label is what the policy asks for.
  - Source: [LinkedIn Professional Community Policies](https://www.linkedin.com/legal/professional-community-policies), LinkedIn, undated, read 2026-10-30, tier 1; [User Agreement](https://www.linkedin.com/legal/user-agreement), LinkedIn, effective 2025-11-03, tier 1; [Updates to User Agreement and Privacy Policy](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a1341216/updates-to-user-agreement-and-privacy-policy), LinkedIn Help Center, updated ≈2026-08, tier 1; [Content credentials](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a6282984), LinkedIn Help Center, updated ≈2026-10, tier 1.
- `rules-10` **Treat profile text as public, and as LinkedIn's AI training data unless the person opts out.** At hand-over, the write phase reminds the person that pasted text is public and adds one line on the Data for Generative AI Improvement setting to `drafts/settings.md`, with this rule's ID. The choice of setting is the person's. Who can see the public profile, and how long search engines take to catch up, are under `url-1` and `url-4`.
  - Why: The User Agreement, section 2.5, says that when members share information on LinkedIn, others can see, copy and use it. LinkedIn's generative AI FAQ says it trains its content-generating AI models on profile data, such as name, photo, positions, education, location, skills, certifications, endorsements and recommendations. Its list also includes jobs-related data: answers to screening questions, and resumes members add to their account for later use. With the Data for Generative AI Improvement setting off, LinkedIn and its affiliates stop using the member's data for that training from then on, but training already done stays. LinkedIn says members in the Designated Countries and the UK have regional rules, set out in its European Regional Privacy Notice; the FAQ does not list those countries. Inference: public text cannot be fully taken back, which is why every draft passes the front-page test before the person pastes it.
  - Source: [User Agreement](https://www.linkedin.com/legal/user-agreement), LinkedIn, effective 2025-11-03, tier 1; [LinkedIn and generative AI (GAI) FAQs](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a5538339), LinkedIn Help Center, updated ≈2026-07, tier 1.
