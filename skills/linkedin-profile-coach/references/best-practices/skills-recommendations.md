# Skills and Recommendations

The audit uses these rules for the Skills and Recommendations rows of its checklist and for each position's linked skills, and the write phase uses them for `drafts/skills.md` and `drafts/recommendation-requests.md`. The conventions (tiers, the ≈ dates, "Inference:") are explained in [best practices](../best-practices.md).

## Skills (`skills-`)

This area covers the Skills section and endorsements: which skills to list and under which names, how they are linked, ordered and featured, and LinkedIn's partner certificates; the audit and the write phase use it. For naming each skill in the text where the work shows it, see `search-2`.

- `skills-1` **List the skills the target roles ask for, when the evidence backs them.** The audit compares the Skills section with the keywords in `targets.md` and the "Skills shown" in the work history documents: `missing` when no skills are listed, `needs work` when an evidence-backed target skill is absent, a listed skill has no evidence, or a listed skill fits no target. The write phase drafts the `skills` block from the audit's keep and add lists, turns each skill without evidence into a question for `work-history-harvest`, and proposes dropping skills that fit no target, minding their endorsements (`skills-7`). Where LinkedIn shows the Skills Match insight, the person opens two or three target postings and notes the job skills it marks as missing (`search-7`).
  - Why: LinkedIn Help says a relevant list of skills helps others understand a member's strengths and matches them with the right opportunities. With no skills listed, the profile shows no Skills section. The profile level meter counts Skills among the seven sections that help a profile appear in search. LinkedIn's Talent Blog (2024) advises listing only skills a member has and leaving out skills that don't fit the roles they want. The Professional Community Policies forbid misleading information about a member's qualifications. On a job post, the Skills Match insight counts how many of the job's skills match the profile, and its ≈2024 Help page says the feature is still rolling out. Job match compares the profile and resume with the job's qualifications. Recruiter Help says recruiters can filter a job's applicants by Skills Match, which marks those whose skills meet a high share of the requirements and whose location fits. A 2024 peer-reviewed study by three LinkedIn employees, on US profiles from 2015 to 2021, found that ten more self-added skills went with a median employment gap about 0.7 months shorter, and ten more endorsed skills with one about 0.4 months shorter, from a median of about 6 months. Returns were larger for more experienced workers. The data are observational.
  - Source: [Add and remove skills on your profile](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a549047), LinkedIn Help Center, updated ≈2026-02, tier 1; [Use the profile level meter to update your profile](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a594698), LinkedIn Help Center, updated ≈2026-02, tier 1; [Why It's Important to List Skills on Your LinkedIn Profile](https://www.linkedin.com/business/talent/blog/talent-acquisition/skills-on-linkedin-profile), LinkedIn Talent Blog, 2024-04-04, tier 1; [LinkedIn Professional Community Policies](https://www.linkedin.com/legal/professional-community-policies), LinkedIn, undated, read 2026-10-30, tier 1; [Skills Match insight on jobs](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a793433), LinkedIn Help Center, updated ≈2024, tier 1; [Find how you match up with jobs on LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a7120158), LinkedIn Help Center, updated ≈2026-02, tier 1; [Skills filter in Recruiter and Recruiter Lite](https://www.linkedin.com/help/recruiter/answer/a593591), LinkedIn Recruiter Help, updated ≈2024, tier 1; [Skill Signals in a Digital Job Search Market and Duration in Employment Gaps](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12122-024-09363-y), Journal of Labor Research (Springer), 2024-10-16, tier 2.
- `skills-2` **Pick each skill from LinkedIn's suggestions, under its standard name.** The audit flags skills whose names look typed in rather than picked, such as long phrases, private abbreviations or several skills in one entry. The write phase drafts each skill under its common name and tells the person to pick the matching suggestion as they type, or the closest one. For a skill that already has endorsements, the person weighs the new name against losing them (`skills-7`).
  - Why: LinkedIn Help's steps for adding a skill have the member type it and pick it from the dropdown or the suggestions. Recruiter Help says search filters match against standardized versions of member profiles, while a keyword search matches a term anywhere on the profile. Recruiter Help also calls the Skills-section skills behind Skills Match standardized, and says the Skills filter offers recruiters the common skills for the job title they enter. LinkedIn Engineering wrote in 2023 that its skills taxonomy maps aliases to standard skills and feeds Recruiter Search and job recommendations. Inference: a skill picked from LinkedIn's list matches the filters most reliably. LinkedIn does not say how it maps a name typed another way.
  - Source: [Add and remove skills on your profile](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a549047), LinkedIn Help Center, updated ≈2026-02, tier 1; [AI Search and Projects in Recruiter FAQ](https://www.linkedin.com/help/recruiter/answer/a1660341), LinkedIn Recruiter Help, updated ≈2026-10, tier 1; [Skills filter in Recruiter and Recruiter Lite](https://www.linkedin.com/help/recruiter/answer/a593591), LinkedIn Recruiter Help, updated ≈2024, tier 1; [Recruiter and Recruiter Lite search filters and definitions](https://www.linkedin.com/help/recruiter/answer/a414428), LinkedIn Recruiter Help, updated ≈2026-09, tier 1; [Building and maintaining the skills taxonomy that powers LinkedIn's Skills Graph](https://www.linkedin.com/blog/engineering/data/building-maintaining-the-skills-taxonomy-that-powers-linkedins-skills-graph), LinkedIn Engineering Blog, 2023-03-21, tier 1.
- `skills-3` **Link each skill to the positions, education and certifications where the person used it.** The audit reads the links from the person's export when it shows them, otherwise from a screenshot of the Skills section or of each position, and marks them `can't tell` without either. The write phase lists in `skills.md`, next to each skill, the entries to tick, each with the evidence ID that shows the skill there. It also lists per position the skills to link, each backed by an approved evidence record.
  - Why: LinkedIn Help's add-skill form lets a member tick where they put the skill to use. Experience and Education entries can each carry skills, and skills added to a position also appear under Skills. LinkedIn's Talent Blog (2024) advises ticking each current and previous role where the skill was used. LinkedIn's notice retiring Skill Assessments points members to tagging skills to their credentials and connecting them to jobs, projects and education instead. Recruiter Help says a skill search shows members with evidence of the skill on their profile, and that a listed skill may be linked to specific experiences or education. Inference: a linked skill shows a recruiter where the person used it. LinkedIn does not say whether linked skills rank higher. LinkedIn Help says the 100-skill limit applies only to the Skills section and does not count skills associated with positions. No page explains how that fits with position skills appearing in the Skills section, so the skill does not present position skills as a way around the limit (`skills-11`). No page states a limit per position. LinkedIn Help lists company name, title, job description, location and employment dates as the Positions data in the data download. Inference: the download does not show which skills are linked to which position.
  - Source: [Add and remove skills on your profile](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a549047), LinkedIn Help Center, updated ≈2026-02, tier 1; [Manage your profile Experience section](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a593695), LinkedIn Help Center, updated ≈2024, tier 1; [Add, edit, or delete education on your profile](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a542784), LinkedIn Help Center, updated ≈2026-03, tier 1; [Why It's Important to List Skills on Your LinkedIn Profile](https://www.linkedin.com/business/talent/blog/talent-acquisition/skills-on-linkedin-profile), LinkedIn Talent Blog, 2024-04-04, tier 1; [Available LinkedIn Skill Assessments](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a507734), LinkedIn Help Center, updated ≈2023, tier 1; [Skills filter in Recruiter and Recruiter Lite](https://www.linkedin.com/help/recruiter/answer/a593591), LinkedIn Recruiter Help, updated ≈2024, tier 1; [Skill endorsements](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a565106), LinkedIn Help Center, updated ≈2026-02, tier 1; [Download your data](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a1339364), LinkedIn Help Center, updated ≈2026-10, tier 1.
- `skills-4` **Put the target skills first in the Skills section.** The audit checks the order on a screenshot of the Skills section. The write phase gives the order in `skills.md`, target skills with verified evidence first, for the person to set in LinkedIn's editor.
  - Why: LinkedIn Help (≈2024) says members can reorder skills by dragging them in a Reorder window. The same page says that with more than three skills LinkedIn sorts them into Industry Knowledge, Tools & Technologies and Interpersonal Skills, and a skill cannot move to another group. That grouping has not been checked against today's editor, and the page does not say how LinkedIn orders skills by default, so the person follows the live editor. LinkedIn's Official Blog advised in 2016 checking that skills are listed in the order of the strengths a member wants to highlight. LinkedIn Help (≈2021) warns that hiding endorsements can change the order. Inference: viewers see the first skills before they open the full list.
  - Source: [Display Order of Skills](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a568137), LinkedIn Help Center, updated ≈2024, tier 1; [Rethinking Endorsements So You Always Look Your Best](https://www.linkedin.com/blog/member/product/rethinking-endorsements-linkedin-features), LinkedIn Official Blog, 2016-10-19, tier 1; [Hide or Show a Skill Endorsement](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a563082), LinkedIn Help Center, updated ≈2021, tier 1.
- `skills-5` **Fill the five Top Skills in About with target skills that have verified evidence, when the editor offers them.** The audit marks Top Skills `can't tell` unless a screenshot of About shows them, and otherwise checks them against the targets. The write phase proposes five skills from the drafted list, verified evidence first, and asks the person whether their About editor offers Top Skills.
  - Why: LinkedIn's Talent Blog said in April 2024 that members can highlight up to five skills in About. It said LinkedIn preloads five based on the rest of the profile, that members can choose any five, and that they edit them with the pencil icon on About. That post is the only source. No Help Center page documents Top Skills, and the same post gives an outdated 50-skill limit, so the feature may have changed. Inference: the preloaded five follow the profile as it was, not the target roles, so the person checks them against the targets.
  - Source: [Why It's Important to List Skills on Your LinkedIn Profile](https://www.linkedin.com/business/talent/blog/talent-acquisition/skills-on-linkedin-profile), LinkedIn Talent Blog, 2024-04-04, tier 1.
- `skills-6` **Add new skills during the search as the person gains them, and name them in the course or certificate entry.** When a work history document holds a course, certificate or project from the break, the write phase adds the skills it shows to the `skills` block with the evidence ID and names them in the entry's text. At hand-over, the agent tells the person to come back when they finish a course or project (`search-8`).
  - Why: A 2024 peer-reviewed study of US LinkedIn profiles found that skills added during an employment break had a substantially stronger link with shorter gaps than skills already on the profile. The data are observational, and the authors are LinkedIn employees. Inference: part of the link may come from who keeps a profile current, so the skill adds only skills the evidence backs. LinkedIn's Talent Blog (2024) advises adding new skills as a member learns them. LinkedIn Help advises keeping the profile updated with the latest experience and skills. Another Help page suggests adding relevant skills and certifications to the profile and resume to raise the job match level. Recruiter Help says a skill search matches skills named in a certificate or course. Inference: a course or certificate from the break should name the skills it covers.
  - Source: [Skill Signals in a Digital Job Search Market and Duration in Employment Gaps](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12122-024-09363-y), Journal of Labor Research (Springer), 2024-10-16, tier 2; [Why It's Important to List Skills on Your LinkedIn Profile](https://www.linkedin.com/business/talent/blog/talent-acquisition/skills-on-linkedin-profile), LinkedIn Talent Blog, 2024-04-04, tier 1; [Profile search appearances](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a1588796), LinkedIn Help Center, updated ≈2026-03, tier 1; [Find how you match up with jobs on LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a7120158), LinkedIn Help Center, updated ≈2026-02, tier 1; [Skills filter in Recruiter and Recruiter Lite](https://www.linkedin.com/help/recruiter/answer/a593591), LinkedIn Recruiter Help, updated ≈2024, tier 1.
- `skills-7` **Keep existing endorsements when editing skills.** The write phase marks each endorsed skill that the draft would delete, rename or translate into English, says its endorsements would be lost. For a skill an approved evidence record backs, it offers reordering instead where that serves the targets. A skill without evidence is not kept for its endorsements: it stays a question for `work-history-harvest` (`skills-1`) until a record backs it. The person decides per skill. When the person wants endorsements, the audit checks on a screenshot that "I want to be endorsed" is set to Yes.
  - Why: LinkedIn Help (≈2023) says removing a skill removes its endorsements, and they come back only if the skill is re-added exactly as it was displayed. LinkedIn Help (≈2026-02) says a skill cannot be switched to another language: removing it and adding it again in the new language permanently deletes its endorsements. Inference: reordering does not remove a skill, so it should keep its endorsements, though no page says so. LinkedIn Help (≈2021) says that setting "I want to be endorsed" to No hides all received endorsements and stops new ones, and that single endorsements can be hidden instead.
  - Source: [Skill endorsements missing from your profile](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a566118/skill-endorsements-missing-from-your-profile?lang=en), LinkedIn Help Center, updated ≈2023, tier 1; [Skill endorsements](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a565106), LinkedIn Help Center, updated ≈2026-02, tier 1; [Opt Out of Endorsements](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a565123), LinkedIn Help Center, updated ≈2021, tier 1; [Hide or Show a Skill Endorsement](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a563082), LinkedIn Help Center, updated ≈2021, tier 1.
- `skills-8` **Present endorsements as one minor signal, and rate endorsement gaps `low`.** The audit reports few or missing endorsements at `low` priority, so they stay out of the five changes that open `audit.md`. When the person asks how much endorsements matter, the agent says the evidence is mixed and that the person cannot add endorsements alone.
  - Why: LinkedIn Help says an endorsement adds to a profile's strength and makes it more likely the member is discovered for opportunities related to the skill. LinkedIn's Talent Blog (2024) says endorsements raise a profile in Recruiter searches. Recruiter Help says endorsements may give recruiters evidence of strength in a skill. None of these gives a mechanism or data. LinkedIn Engineering's 2019 account of Recruiter ranking names how closely experience and skills match the search, not endorsements, and Recruiter has changed since. In the 2024 study of US profiles, ten more endorsed skills went with a gap about 0.4 months shorter, against about 0.7 months for ten more self-added skills. A 2017 peer-reviewed online survey of 120 professionals worldwide raises the concern that people endorse others on personal rather than knowledge-based grounds, and its abstract gives no figures. A 2022 review of cybervetting research reports, citing earlier studies, that recruiters check LinkedIn for endorsements and recommendations. The sources disagree on how much endorsements count, so the skill presents them as one signal among several.
  - Source: [Skill endorsements](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a565106), LinkedIn Help Center, updated ≈2026-02, tier 1; [Why It's Important to List Skills on Your LinkedIn Profile](https://www.linkedin.com/business/talent/blog/talent-acquisition/skills-on-linkedin-profile), LinkedIn Talent Blog, 2024-04-04, tier 1; [Skills filter in Recruiter and Recruiter Lite](https://www.linkedin.com/help/recruiter/answer/a593591), LinkedIn Recruiter Help, updated ≈2024, tier 1; [The AI Behind LinkedIn Recruiter Search and Recommendation Systems](https://www.linkedin.com/blog/engineering/recommendations/ai-behind-linkedin-recruiter-search-and-recommendation-systems), LinkedIn Engineering Blog, 2019-04-22, tier 1; [Skill Signals in a Digital Job Search Market and Duration in Employment Gaps](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12122-024-09363-y), Journal of Labor Research (Springer), 2024-10-16, tier 2; [The LinkedIn Endorsement Game: Why and How Professionals Attribute Skills to Others](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1163129), Business and Professional Communication Quarterly (ERIC record), 2017-12, tier 2; [Is cybervetting valuable?](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/identifier/S1754942622000281/type/journal_article), Industrial and Organizational Psychology (Cambridge University Press), 2022-09-09, tier 2.
- `skills-9` **Ask a few former colleagues who saw the work to endorse listed target skills.** The write phase adds one line to a recommendation request, or drafts a short message, that names two or three listed target skills the colleague saw in use. It may recall the theme of praise from a review, never a quote, and the person sends the message. When the person endorses colleagues, they pick skills they saw those colleagues use.
  - Why: LinkedIn Help's job-search best practices advise asking connections to endorse a member's skills. LinkedIn Help says 1st-degree connections endorse the skills on a profile, and that a member does not need to request an endorsement to receive one. No Help page read describes a button for requesting one. Connections can endorse only skills already listed on the profile. LinkedIn's Talent Blog (2024) suggests asking managers and colleagues, and reminding them of similar praise they gave in an annual review or 360 feedback. LinkedIn's Official Blog said in 2014 that an endorsement from an existing colleague is worth more to a recruiter than a similar one from a high-school friend. In 2012 it encouraged members to focus the endorsements they give on skills they can personally attest to.
  - Source: [Find jobs on LinkedIn – Best Practices](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a509392), LinkedIn Help Center, updated ≈2026-03, tier 1; [Skill endorsements](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a565106), LinkedIn Help Center, updated ≈2026-02, tier 1; [Endorse Someone's Skills](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a566100), LinkedIn Help Center, updated ≈2021, tier 1; [Why It's Important to List Skills on Your LinkedIn Profile](https://www.linkedin.com/business/talent/blog/talent-acquisition/skills-on-linkedin-profile), LinkedIn Talent Blog, 2024-04-04, tier 1; [Why LinkedIn Recommendations and Endorsements Should Matter To You](https://www.linkedin.com/blog/member/product/why-linkedin-recommendations-and-endorsements-should-matter-to-you), LinkedIn Official Blog, 2014-09-12, tier 1; [Endorse and be Endorsed](https://www.linkedin.com/blog/member/archive/endorse-and-be-endorsed), LinkedIn Official Blog, 2012-12-18, tier 1.
- `skills-10` **Tell the person about LinkedIn's partner certificates and connected apps for tools they really use.** The audit asks whether the person uses a partner tool on a personal account; nothing is `missing` without one. When the evidence shows an AI tool or app the person used regularly, the write phase tells them they can check whether its maker offers a LinkedIn certificate or a connected app; both are based on actual use, come from personal accounts only and go next to the evidence-backed text, never in its place. Before showing a connected app, the person reads its capability statement against the front-page test and removes the app if it fails.
  - Why: LinkedIn announced in January 2026 that partners such as Descript, Lovable, Relay.app and Replit can confirm a member's skill with their AI tools from real usage. Each partner issues a certificate that the member may choose to share, and the feature is global and in English. LinkedIn Help (≈2026-08) says connecting a tool gives the member a capability statement, a structured description based on how they use it, and that the profile updates automatically as capabilities change. The page lists a related task for removing a connected app, and it does not say who writes the statement or whether the member can edit it. Inference: the app, not the person, supplies the statement, so it needs the front-page test before it goes public. Inference: a statement built from activity on a work account can describe employer work, so the skill suggests connecting personal accounts only. Neither source says whether certificates or statements feed Skills Match, job match or Recruiter search. A 2022 field experiment in South Africa found that giving job seekers skill-assessment results they could credibly share with employers raised their employment and earnings. Inference: proof from real use may weigh more than a self-description, which was not tested on LinkedIn.
  - Source: [LinkedIn Gives Professionals the Edge with Verified Skills and Tools to Navigate the Job Search](https://news.linkedin.com/2026/Professional_Edge_Skills_Verified), LinkedIn Newsroom, 2026-01-26, tier 1; [Connected apps](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a9616064), LinkedIn Help Center, updated ≈2026-08, tier 1; [Job Search and Hiring with Limited Information about Workseekers' Skills](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.20200961), American Economic Review, 2022-11, tier 2.
- `skills-11` **Keep the Skills list within LinkedIn's limit of 100.** The audit counts the listed skills, and the write phase keeps the `skills` block at 100 or fewer, which `check_drafts.py` enforces. No LinkedIn source gives an ideal number, so relevance and evidence set the length (`skills-1`).
  - Why: LinkedIn Help says members can add up to 100 skills, and that the limit applies only to the Skills section: skills tied to Experience roles are not counted. Another Help page says skills added to a position also appear under Skills. No page explains how both hold, so the skill does not offer position skills as a way past the limit. LinkedIn's Talent Blog post of April 2024 still says 50, and the newer Help pages decide.
  - Source: [Add and remove skills on your profile](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a549047), LinkedIn Help Center, updated ≈2026-02, tier 1; [Skill endorsements](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a565106), LinkedIn Help Center, updated ≈2026-02, tier 1; [Manage your profile Experience section](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a593695), LinkedIn Help Center, updated ≈2024, tier 1; [Why It's Important to List Skills on Your LinkedIn Profile](https://www.linkedin.com/business/talent/blog/talent-acquisition/skills-on-linkedin-profile), LinkedIn Talent Blog, 2024-04-04, tier 1.
- `skills-12` **Show skills through the profile itself, since Skill Assessments are retired.** The write phase leaves skill assessments and their badges out of every suggestion. When the person asks about them, the agent says LinkedIn retired them and points to linked skills (`skills-3`) and partner certificates (`skills-10`).
  - Why: LinkedIn Help (≈2023) says Skill Assessments are no longer available and that their badges were to be removed from profiles in 2024. It encourages members to show their skills on the profile instead, and a second Help page on the retirement says hirers value examples of how a candidate applied a skill. LinkedIn's "Create a good LinkedIn profile" page (≈2026-02) still suggests taking assessments, and the skill treats that line as stale.
  - Source: [Skill Assessments - no longer available](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a1690529), LinkedIn Help Center, updated ≈2023, tier 1; [LinkedIn Skill Assessments](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a507663), LinkedIn Help Center, updated ≈2023, tier 1; [Create a good LinkedIn profile](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a554351), LinkedIn Help Center, updated ≈2026-02, tier 1.

## Recommendations (`recommendations-`)

This area covers LinkedIn recommendations: whom to ask, how a request works and its limits, what to do with the ones that arrive, and giving one in return; the audit, the write phase and the [recommendation procedure](../recommendations.md) use it.

- `recommendations-1` **Ask for recommendations from people who saw the person's work first-hand.** The audit lists the received recommendations, gets each writer's relationship and the position covered from the person or a screenshot, and names writers in `audit.md` only by relationship. It marks the item `missing` when there are none and `needs work` when none covers the current or last position. The write phase follows the [recommendation procedure](../recommendations.md) to choose writers among managers, peers, people the person supported and partner teams, and checks with the person before suggesting a client.
  - Why: LinkedIn Help's job-search best practices advise asking connections for a recommendation. LinkedIn Help describes a recommendation as recognizing a connection such as a colleague, business partner or student. LinkedIn's Official Blog advised in 2014 starting with people who know the member well and can speak to their ability in their field. A 2020 resume audit study in South Africa found that adding a reference letter from a former employer raised callbacks by 60 percent. It covered a low-skill labor market, women drove the effect, and the letters were not LinkedIn recommendations. A 2022 review of cybervetting research reports, citing earlier studies, that recruiters check LinkedIn for recommendations and endorsements. In a 2019 study of 615 ICT professionals at one Spanish company, the mean number of recommendations received was 0.32. Inference: even a few specific recommendations set a tech profile apart, and one tied to the last position speaks to the work the person now seeks. No study tested LinkedIn recommendations in tech hiring. Inference: a recommendation shows who wrote it, so one from a client can reveal the client, and the person checks that against their contract.
  - Source: [Find jobs on LinkedIn – Best Practices](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a509392), LinkedIn Help Center, updated ≈2026-03, tier 1; [Create a good LinkedIn profile](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a554351), LinkedIn Help Center, updated ≈2026-02, tier 1; [Why LinkedIn Recommendations and Endorsements Should Matter To You](https://www.linkedin.com/blog/member/product/why-linkedin-recommendations-and-endorsements-should-matter-to-you), LinkedIn Official Blog, 2014-09-12, tier 1; [The Value of Reference Letters: Experimental Evidence from South Africa](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/app.20180666), American Economic Journal: Applied Economics, 2020-07, tier 2; [Is cybervetting valuable?](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/identifier/S1754942622000281/type/journal_article), Industrial and Organizational Psychology (Cambridge University Press), 2022-09-09, tier 2; [LinkedIn "Big Four": Job Performance Validation in the ICT Sector](https://journals.copmadrid.org/jwop/art/jwop2019a7), Journal of Work and Organizational Psychology, 2019, tier 2.
- `recommendations-2` **Connect on LinkedIn with each chosen writer before asking.** The write phase confirms with the person that every chosen writer is a 1st-degree connection. For anyone who is not, the person sends a connection request first, while work email and chat still reach them.
  - Why: LinkedIn Help says members can request recommendations from 1st-degree connections they work or worked with, and can write one only for a 1st-degree connection. A recommendation disappears when either person removes the connection, and reconnecting does not bring it back. Inference: colleagues are easiest to reach while the person still has work email and chat, so connecting comes first.
  - Source: [Recommendations on LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a541653), LinkedIn Help Center, updated ≈2023, tier 1; [Recommend someone on LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a543723/recommend-someone-on-linkedin?lang=en), LinkedIn Help Center, updated ≈2024, tier 1.
- `recommendations-3` **Ask each writer for specific examples of shared work and its results.** Each request names two or three concrete, clean things the writer saw the person do, with results where the evidence has them. Recommendations the person writes for others follow the same pattern.
  - Why: LinkedIn's Official Blog called "show, don't tell" the golden rule of recommendations in 2014: describe how the person worked instead of praising them in general. LinkedIn Sales Solutions says a solid recommendation describes how the two people collaborated and gives specific examples of how the client benefited. That page is written for sales profiles. A 2014 field experiment that hired 952 entry-level workers at random on an online labor market found that detailed public evaluations improved their later employment outcomes more than coarse ones. Inference: a specific recommendation carries more weight than a generic one, which was not tested on LinkedIn.
  - Source: [Why LinkedIn Recommendations and Endorsements Should Matter To You](https://www.linkedin.com/blog/member/product/why-linkedin-recommendations-and-endorsements-should-matter-to-you), LinkedIn Official Blog, 2014-09-12, tier 1; [Create an effective sales profile: Step Nine, Solicit recommendations](https://business.linkedin.com/sales-solutions/cx/22/05/want-to-generate-more-leads-and-opportunities-for-dialogue/solicit-recommendations), LinkedIn Sales Solutions, undated, read 2026-10-30, tier 1; [Inefficient Hiring in Entry-Level Labor Markets](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.104.11.3565), American Economic Review, 2014-11, tier 2.
- `recommendations-4` **Send each request through LinkedIn's Request a recommendation flow, with a personal message, the right relationship and the right position.** The write phase gives the person the steps with the drafted message, and asks them to check the relationship, the position and their primary profile language before sending. It finishes the person's Experience entries first, so the position they need is there to choose.
  - Why: LinkedIn Help (≈2025-11) says to open the writer's profile, click More and select Request a recommendation. A member's own profile shows a Recommendations section only once a visible recommendation exists, so a first request starts from the writer's profile. The form asks for the Relationship and the Position at the time, and lets the member add a personalized message. Help gives no length limit for that message. The relationship cannot be changed after sending: the writer has to delete the request before a new one can go out. Requests go out only in the member's primary profile language. LinkedIn Sales Solutions advises a personalized message with every request. Inference: the Position list seems to come from the person's own Experience entries, though LinkedIn does not say so.

- `recommendations-5` **Offer each writer a recommendation of their own.** The write phase puts an offer to write one into each request, or drafts the person's recommendation to send first, from what the person saw the writer do and with `[Name]` as the placeholder. Each such draft goes through the NDA pass like any other, and the person sends it through LinkedIn's Recommend flow.
  - Why: LinkedIn Sales Solutions advises offering recommendations to others before asking them for one. LinkedIn Help says a member can recommend a 1st-degree connection without being asked. They open the connection's profile, click More, select Recommend, fill in Relationship and Position at the time, click Next, then write the text and send it. Inference: a recommendation helps the colleague too, so an exchange suits both.

- `recommendations-6` **Plan requests around LinkedIn's limits: one open request per writer, and one recommendation per writer and position.** The write phase uses the audit's list of received recommendations to ask each writer only for a position they have not covered, the most recent shared position first. It sends no second request to a writer while one is open, and a writer who does not answer may have declined, so the person can ask someone else.
  - Why: LinkedIn Help (≈2025-11) says a member can have only one open request to a connection at a time, with no limit on the total they request or receive. LinkedIn Help (≈2023) says a member can request or give only one recommendation per position, per member. An older page (≈2021) says one per person. The skill plans with one per position and tells the person the pages disagree. The person asked does not have to respond, and LinkedIn does not tell the sender about a decline. Inference: the most recent position is closest to the work the person now seeks.

- `recommendations-7` **Check each recommendation as public text before accepting it, and ask for a revision instead of dismissing one that needs changes.** The person pastes each pending recommendation, and the agent checks it in the conversation against the front-page test, the evidence and the targets, saving none of its text to the workspace. When it needs changes, the agent drafts a short, clean revision note that names them. The audit applies the same check to recommendations already on the profile.
  - Why: LinkedIn Help says a member can accept, dismiss or ask the writer to revise a recommendation. The Help pages describe no way for the recipient to edit the text. A revision request can carry a personal message asking for specific changes, and the revised text appears only after the recipient reviews it. A dismissed recommendation cannot be retrieved, and the writer must delete it before the member can request a new one. Accepting adds the recommendation to the profile and sends an update to the member's network. LinkedIn Help (≈2024) says received recommendations are visible to signed-in 1st-, 2nd- and 3rd-degree connections. The same page says the public profile shows how many people recommended the member and up to two received recommendations. So the skill treats every recommendation as readable by anyone.

- `recommendations-8` **Hide received recommendations that don't support the target roles.** The audit lists each received recommendation with its fit to the targets, and suggests hiding those that don't fit and those that fail the front-page test when the writer cannot revise them. The person decides, and hiding can be undone.
  - Why: LinkedIn Help (≈2026-02) says an accepted recommendation is visible to the member's network by default, and suggests hiding recommendations that don't suit the member's professional goals. LinkedIn Help says recommendations appear in chronological order and cannot be reordered, so hiding is the only display control. A received recommendation is hidden with an On/Off toggle on the Received tab, and it can be shown again. Inference: hiding is also the fallback for a recommendation that fails the front-page test when its writer cannot be reached.
