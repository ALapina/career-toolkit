# Headline and About

The audit uses these rules for the Headline and About rows of its checklist, and the write phase uses them for `drafts/headline.md` and `drafts/about.md`. The conventions (tiers, the ≈ dates, "Inference:") are explained in [best practices](../best-practices.md).

## Headline (`headline-`)

The headline is the line under the person's name. The audit checks it with these rules and the `search-` rules, and the write phase drafts it in two or three variants.

- `headline-1` **Name the target role's standard title in the headline, with the skills the evidence backs.** The audit checks that the headline names the target role in its standard form, not only an in-house title, a slogan or an employer, and that it carries two or three skills or specialties from `targets.md`. The write phase puts the title in every variant and backs each skill with evidence, verified where possible. A role the person has not held yet follows `headline-4`.
  - Why: LinkedIn Help says the headline is displayed in search results; that page was last updated about five years ago. LinkedIn's 2020 advice to job seekers is to use a standard job title and headline that recruiters would search for. LinkedIn Help says standard job titles are what members search for. LinkedIn's Search appearances page advises adding relevant keywords to the headline. Skills Match counts skills it extracts from the headline. The jobs LinkedIn recommends as matching the profile draw partly on the headline. Recruiter's Job titles filter reads Experience titles and does not name the headline. Inference: the headline works through keyword search, skill extraction and what searchers see. Inference: after a layoff, with no current title, the headline is where the target title goes.
  - Source: [Edit Your Headline](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a542926/edit-your-headline?lang=en), LinkedIn Help, updated ≈2021, tier 1; [To Find Your Next Job More Quickly, Tell Your Community You're Open to Work](https://www.linkedin.com/blog/member/product/the-benefits-of-sharing-with-your-community-you-re-open-to-work), LinkedIn Official Blog, 2020-10-29, tier 1; [Order of your profile in people search results](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a521944), LinkedIn Help, updated ≈2023, tier 1; [Profile search appearances](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a1588796), LinkedIn Help, updated ≈2026-03, tier 1; [Skills Match insight on jobs](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a793433), LinkedIn Help, updated ≈2024, tier 1; [Job recommendations based on your preferences and profile](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a512279), LinkedIn Help, updated ≈2026-05, tier 1; [Recruiter and Recruiter Lite search filters and definitions](https://www.linkedin.com/help/recruiter/answer/a414428), LinkedIn Recruiter Help, updated ≈2026-09, tier 1.
- `headline-2` **When the last role has ended, rewrite a headline that still presents it as current.** The audit marks a headline that presents the ended role as current, such as "QA Lead at Contoso", as `needs work` with priority high. The write phase replaces it with the variants from `headline-5`, and a status phrase such as "open to new roles" goes in only if the person wants it.
  - Why: LinkedIn Help says the headline is usually created when a member adds a new current position, and that it can differ from the title of the current position. Inference: after a layoff, a headline created that way keeps naming the old job until the person rewrites it, and it then claims work that has ended (`headline-4`). LinkedIn Help also says the headline can state another professional status, for example being unemployed. It gives no data on whether that helps, so the skill leaves the choice to the person. That Help page was last updated about five years ago. The Open to Work signal is covered by `opentowork-`.
  - Source: [Edit Your Headline](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a542926/edit-your-headline?lang=en), LinkedIn Help, updated ≈2021, tier 1.
- `headline-3` **Put the most important words first, within 220 characters.** The audit checks what the first words of the headline say and that the target title appears early. The write phase opens each variant with its main point and keeps the target title close to the start. It checks the length with `scripts/check_drafts.py`, and the person confirms it with the counter in LinkedIn's editor.
  - Why: LinkedIn's Talent Blog says a headline can use up to 220 characters but may be truncated in some LinkedIn features. It advises keeping the headline clear and concise, with the most important information at the beginning. No Help Center page states the limit or where the cut falls. Inference: a title close to the start is the one most likely to stay visible when the headline is cut. A 2021 Nature study of Switzerland's public employment service job platform found that the quarter of recruiters who decided fastest viewed a jobseeker's profile for 5.7 seconds on average, and the slowest quarter for 31.7 seconds. The platform is not LinkedIn. Inference: in a few seconds a reader sees little beyond the top of the profile, so the headline's first words carry the most weight.
  - Source: [How Talent Professionals Can Grow Their LinkedIn Networks](https://www.linkedin.com/business/talent/blog/talent-acquisition/how-to-grow-linkedin-network), LinkedIn Talent Blog, 2025-04-03, tier 1; [10 LinkedIn Profile Headlines to Inspire Your Own](https://www.linkedin.com/business/talent/blog/product-tips/recruiters-with-eye-catching-linkedin-profile-headlines), LinkedIn Talent Blog, 2025-02-27, tier 1; [Monitoring hiring discrimination through online recruitment platforms](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-020-03136-0), Nature (Springer Nature), 2021-01-20, tier 2.
- `headline-4` **Claim only a role and a level the evidence supports.** The audit compares the role and level words in the headline, such as Senior, Lead or Head of, with the Experience titles and the evidence records. The write phase names a role the person has not held yet as the next step ("moving into data analysis"), never as a current title, so every claim stays defensible.
  - Why: LinkedIn's Professional Community Policies forbid posting misleading or deceptive information about your qualifications, work experience or achievements. In interviews with 61 HR professionals in two US metro areas (2014 to 2016, published 2021), most said they reviewed applicants' information on LinkedIn. A 2022 review of cybervetting research, which also draws on interviews with 77 US hiring agents, reports that checking whether LinkedIn matches the resume is a common practice. Inference: a headline that claims more than the resume or the Experience section is likely to be noticed, and the person has to defend it in an interview.
  - Source: [LinkedIn Professional Community Policies](https://www.linkedin.com/legal/professional-community-policies), LinkedIn Legal, undated, read 2026-10-30, tier 1; [The hunt for red flags: cybervetting as morally performative practice](https://academic.oup.com/ser/article/20/3/915/6132113), Socio-Economic Review (Oxford University Press), 2021-02-10, tier 2; [Is cybervetting valuable?](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/identifier/S1754942622000281/type/journal_article), Industrial and Organizational Psychology (Cambridge University Press), 2022-09-09, tier 2.
- `headline-5` **Offer two or three variants: one title-led, one value-led.** The write phase drafts a title-led variant that opens with the target title (`headline-1`) and a value-led variant that opens with what the person does, for whom and with which evidence-backed result, then names the title. A third variant that leads with the domain is optional. The person picks one, and every variant names the target role.
  - Why: LinkedIn's guidance points two ways. Its 2020 advice to job seekers and its Help Center say to use standard job titles that people search for. Its Talent Blog, writing in 2025 about recruiters' own headlines, says to go beyond the job title, which is displayed below the headline anyway, and to treat the headline like a mission statement. No source says to draft several versions. Inference: two angles let the person choose between the two pieces of advice. Search appearances then shows which job titles they are found for (`search-9`). The value-led form is the skill's reading of the mission-statement advice. No LinkedIn page says what shows below the headline when there is no current position. Inference: after a layoff the person cannot count on that line, so every variant names the role. Whether edits notify the network is covered by `settings-10`.
  - Source: [10 LinkedIn Profile Headlines to Inspire Your Own](https://www.linkedin.com/business/talent/blog/product-tips/recruiters-with-eye-catching-linkedin-profile-headlines), LinkedIn Talent Blog, 2025-02-27, tier 1; [To Find Your Next Job More Quickly, Tell Your Community You're Open to Work](https://www.linkedin.com/blog/member/product/the-benefits-of-sharing-with-your-community-you-re-open-to-work), LinkedIn Official Blog, 2020-10-29, tier 1; [Order of your profile in people search results](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a521944), LinkedIn Help, updated ≈2023, tier 1; [Profile search appearances](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a1588796), LinkedIn Help, updated ≈2026-03, tier 1.

## About (`about-`)

About is the person's own summary on the profile. The audit checks it starting with the part shown before "see more", and the write phase drafts it from approved evidence. Any mention of the former employer or the layoff in About follows `career-break-4`.

- `about-1` **Put the strongest point in the opening lines.** The audit judges the opening from the person's screenshot of the About section as others see it, because no LinkedIn page says how much text shows before "see more". The write phase opens with the person's role, specialty and one result, verified where possible, and drafts two or three openings that take different angles.
  - Why: LinkedIn's Talent Blog says every word of the summary matters but the first words matter most, because readers who are not hooked right away are lost. No LinkedIn page documents where "see more" cuts the text, so the skill quotes no number. The 2021 Nature study of a Swiss job platform in `headline-3` found average profile-viewing times from 5.7 seconds, for the quarter of recruiters who decided fastest, to 31.7 seconds for the slowest. Inference: many readers see only the opening.
  - Source: [14 LinkedIn Profile Summaries That We Love (And How to Boost Your Own)](https://www.linkedin.com/business/talent/blog/product-tips/linkedin-profile-summaries-that-we-love-and-how-to-boost-your-own), LinkedIn Talent Blog, 2024-04-30, tier 1; [Monitoring hiring discrimination through online recruitment platforms](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-020-03136-0), Nature (Springer Nature), 2021-01-20, tier 2.
- `about-2` **Say which roles the person wants, and end with an ask.** The audit checks that About says what the person does, what they are good at and what they are looking for next. The write phase names the target roles in plain words and closes with one ask that fits the goal, such as an invitation to get in touch about those roles. If the person is still employed, the write phase asks before About mentions the search.
  - Why: LinkedIn Help says the About section should express your mission, motivation and skills. Its job-search best practices say to use the summary to share what types of opportunities you are pursuing. LinkedIn's Talent Blog says an invitation to connect is a good way to end, and that the ask can follow your goal. Inference: About is visible to everyone who opens the profile, including a current employer, so a person who is searching quietly decides whether it names the search. The private signal to recruiters is covered by `opentowork-`.
  - Source: [Create a good LinkedIn profile](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a554351), LinkedIn Help, updated ≈2026-02, tier 1; [Find jobs on LinkedIn – Best Practices](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a509392), LinkedIn Help, updated ≈2026-03, tier 1; [14 LinkedIn Profile Summaries That We Love (And How to Boost Your Own)](https://www.linkedin.com/business/talent/blog/product-tips/linkedin-profile-summaries-that-we-love-and-how-to-boost-your-own), LinkedIn Talent Blog, 2024-04-30, tier 1.
- `about-3` **Back the story with the person's biggest results across roles, with numbers the evidence supports.** The audit flags an About that lists duties or traits and no results. The write phase picks the two or three strongest records, verified where possible, and combines results from different roles when they show one theme. It states numbers as the person's own relative results, so the text stays clean.
  - Why: LinkedIn's Talent Blog advises citing the biggest takeaway from your Experience section and combining accomplishments across roles where you can. It praises an example summary for including numbers as proof. It suggests a line of the form "I've made X in the past X years", with hires, sales and acquisitions as its sample measures. This is editorial advice; the research found no tested study showing that numbers beat lists of duties. The skill swaps the sample measures for the person's own results, because the employer's revenue, volumes and client counts stay out of clean text (SKILL.md).
  - Source: [14 LinkedIn Profile Summaries That We Love (And How to Boost Your Own)](https://www.linkedin.com/business/talent/blog/product-tips/linkedin-profile-summaries-that-we-love-and-how-to-boost-your-own), LinkedIn Talent Blog, 2024-04-30, tier 1.
- `about-4` **Name the target role's top skills in About, each next to its evidence.** The audit checks About against the keep and add keyword lists. The write phase names each top skill in a sentence about the work it served, and may close with a short Specialties line of evidence-backed terms if the person wants one. Top skills shown with About belong to `skills-5`.
  - Why: LinkedIn's Talent Blog says to include keywords that highlight your top skills to improve your search rank on LinkedIn and Google. It also suggests listing specialties at the end of the summary. LinkedIn's Search appearances page says to add relevant keywords to your summary. `search-2` explains how skill searches read the summary, and `search-3` keeps each term to once or twice. LinkedIn Help says that turning off the public profile hides it from search engine results, so the Google part depends on the public profile settings (`url-1`).
  - Source: [14 LinkedIn Profile Summaries That We Love (And How to Boost Your Own)](https://www.linkedin.com/business/talent/blog/product-tips/linkedin-profile-summaries-that-we-love-and-how-to-boost-your-own), LinkedIn Talent Blog, 2024-04-30, tier 1; [Profile search appearances](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a1588796), LinkedIn Help, updated ≈2026-03, tier 1; [LinkedIn public profile visibility](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a518980), LinkedIn Help, updated ≈2026-10, tier 1.
- `about-5` **Show each strength with an example instead of an adjective.** The audit flags self-descriptions such as "strategic", "motivated" or "creative" that no example in the text supports. The write phase replaces each one with the evidence that shows it, or drops it.
  - Why: LinkedIn's Talent Blog advises avoiding overused words that have lost their meaning, naming strategic, motivated and creative. It suggests showing those traits with an example or a quick story instead. This gives the house style in `style-2` and `style-3` a LinkedIn source for About.
  - Source: [14 LinkedIn Profile Summaries That We Love (And How to Boost Your Own)](https://www.linkedin.com/business/talent/blog/product-tips/linkedin-profile-summaries-that-we-love-and-how-to-boost-your-own), LinkedIn Talent Blog, 2024-04-30, tier 1.
- `about-6` **Keep About to one or two paragraphs, or bullet points, within 2,600 characters.** The audit flags a long, unbroken block of text. The write phase breaks the text up, checks the length with `scripts/check_drafts.py`, and asks the person to confirm it with the counter in LinkedIn's editor.
  - Why: LinkedIn Help says that, ideally, the About text should be limited to one or two paragraphs. It adds that bullet points work for people who are not comfortable writing paragraphs. LinkedIn's Talent Blog gives a 2,600-character maximum and says people skim the summary, so the text should be broken up. No Help Center page states the limit, so the editor's counter decides.
  - Source: [Create a good LinkedIn profile](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a554351), LinkedIn Help, updated ≈2026-02, tier 1; [14 LinkedIn Profile Summaries That We Love (And How to Boost Your Own)](https://www.linkedin.com/business/talent/blog/product-tips/linkedin-profile-summaries-that-we-love-and-how-to-boost-your-own), LinkedIn Talent Blog, 2024-04-30, tier 1.
- `about-7` **Write About in the first person.** The audit flags an About written in the third person. The write phase drafts in the first person, in the person's own voice (`style-5`).
  - Why: LinkedIn's Talent Blog advises writing the summary the way you would speak, which it says means using the first person. This gives the first-person half of `style-1` a LinkedIn source.
  - Source: [14 LinkedIn Profile Summaries That We Love (And How to Boost Your Own)](https://www.linkedin.com/business/talent/blog/product-tips/linkedin-profile-summaries-that-we-love-and-how-to-boost-your-own), LinkedIn Talent Blog, 2024-04-30, tier 1.
